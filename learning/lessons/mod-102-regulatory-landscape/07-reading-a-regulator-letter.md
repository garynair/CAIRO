# Chapter 7 — Reading a Regulator Letter

## Why this chapter exists

Regulators rarely ask the question they actually want
answered. The letter you receive is a **probe**: it tests
what your organisation looks like when you have thirty
days to produce evidence, how mature your governance
posture is, and whether you can be trusted to police
yourself for the next twelve months. The literal
enumerated questions are the visible surface of the
probe, not its substance.

The most common CAIRO error is treating a regulator letter
as a questionnaire and answering each item literally. That
approach passes the *"we responded on time"* test and
fails everything downstream — the supervisor develops the
follow-up question the letter was designed to surface, and
now the follow-up is on the record.

This chapter builds the skill of reading past the literal
text. Exercise 04 puts you in front of a realistic
supervisor letter and asks you to reverse-engineer it.

## What a regulator letter actually is

Three things simultaneously:

1. **A record-building exercise.** Whatever you write goes
   into the supervisory file for the institution.
   Every claim becomes part of the record the regulator
   will read against the next incident. Words matter.
2. **A capability test.** The regulator is watching not
   just *what* you produce but *how quickly* and *how
   organised*. An institution that produces a coherent
   response in twenty days demonstrates governance maturity;
   one that scrambles for the full thirty days and still
   produces something disjointed reveals something the
   examiner will remember.
3. **A framing negotiation.** The letter sets one frame
   for the conversation; your response has one chance to
   reframe. Frames are sticky. If the letter says
   *"customer-service AI capability"* and you accept that
   language, everything after runs on that frame. If you
   respond with *"the customer-service model, which is
   supervised under our MRM framework"*, you have moved
   the frame to a supervisory vocabulary the regulator
   respects.

## The four questions to ask of any regulator letter

Before you draft a single word of response, work through
these four questions in order.

### Question 1 — What is the underlying concern?

The literal items in the letter are almost never the
whole concern. Ask:

- Why is this letter arriving *now* and not six months
  ago?
- What recent event — public announcement, incident,
  supervisory guidance, peer institution's fine — most
  plausibly prompted the letter?
- What worry, one sentence long, would the examiner
  reveal if you sat down with them off the record?

The underlying concern is usually one of a small set:
*"is your MRM framework actually covering this AI?"*,
*"is the disclosure to customers adequate?"*, *"is a peer
institution's failure something we should worry about at
you too?"*, *"is your governance operating in reality or
only on paper?"* Pick the concern that best fits the
timing and the questions asked.

### Question 2 — What posture wins?

What kind of responding institution gets through this
review without follow-up? Two dimensions to reason about:

- **Substantive posture** — an institution that has
  clearly integrated AI into its existing risk framework,
  can name its material systems, can produce evidence
  quickly, and knows what it does not yet know.
- **Tonal posture** — an institution that is candid about
  what is in flight, does not oversell what is in place,
  does not adopt marketing language, and answers questions
  the regulator did not think to ask when the answer would
  matter.

The winning posture is almost never *"we have everything
under control."* Regulators do not believe that claim; the
attempt to make it triggers the deeper probe.

### Question 3 — What posture must be avoided?

What kind of response *guarantees* follow-up? The
recurring patterns:

- **Defensive** — quoting language back at the regulator,
  arguing about scope, refusing to answer what was not
  literally asked.
- **Overpromising** — claiming controls exist that a
  follow-up would find aren't operational.
- **Non-substantive** — long response, thin content. The
  examiner reads the whole thing, gets nothing they can
  test, follows up.
- **Silent on the obvious question** — if the letter is
  clearly probing MRM coverage of AI, a response that does
  not address MRM at all makes the underlying concern the
  central finding.

Naming the anti-posture explicitly in your response plan
keeps the drafting team disciplined.

### Question 4 — What is the lede sentence?

The lede is the one sentence at the top of your cover
letter that the examiner uses to frame everything else.
Every response has one whether you author it deliberately
or not; author it deliberately.

A well-authored lede:

- Names the responding institution and the regulatory
  frame you accept (or reframe to).
- Signals substantive engagement in one clause.
- Does not oversell.
- Sets up the structure of what follows.

Example (adapted from Exercise 04): *"Trillium Bancorp
takes the OCC's inquiry seriously and has treated the
enumerated items as an occasion to formalize the MRM
coverage of our AI/ML deployments alongside our response;
what follows is organized by the letter's enumeration
with an added Section A on the MRM framework."*

That sentence does four things at once — accepts the
supervisor's authority, states the frame (MRM), signals
that the institution has done more than the minimum, and
tells the examiner what to expect next.

## Things to *not* say

Regulator responses are not free of choices about
disclosure. Two categories deserve deliberate handling:

- **True facts that are not load-bearing for the
  question.** A regulator is entitled to accurate answers
  to the questions asked. They are not entitled to
  volunteered material that raises new concerns.
  Disclosing that a prior incident was investigated and
  closed is different from volunteering a list of every
  near-miss the model has ever had.
- **In-flight work that could be misread as an admission
  of prior gap.** *"We are updating our MRM framework to
  explicitly cover AI systems"* can be read as *"our MRM
  framework did not cover AI systems"* — which may be
  literally true and unhelpful to say in a response that
  will sit in the supervisory file.

The rule is not to lie. The rule is to answer the
questions asked with substance and precision, to
volunteer what materially aids the regulator's
understanding, and to avoid volunteering what would raise
new questions the regulator has not yet thought to ask.
Everything you say goes into the record. Say what you
mean and be prepared to defend it — but do not say
anything you have not decided to defend.

Exercise 04 asks you to name two "things you will not
say." The exercise is a discipline, not a permission to
withhold what a regulator is entitled to know.

## The one-page response plan

Before drafting the response itself, produce a one-page
plan. The plan has these sections:

```
### Underlying concern
    - One sentence — the probe as best you can read it.

### Winning posture
    - Two sentences — what "good" looks like for this letter.

### Anti-posture
    - Bullet list — the postures the response will avoid.

### Lede sentence
    - The literal sentence, drafted.

### Internal mobilization
    - Who produces what, on what cadence, over N days.

### Scope decisions
    - What we treat as in-scope beyond the literal enumeration.
    - What we treat as out-of-scope and why.

### Requests to the regulator
    - What (if anything) we ask back — clarification,
      extension, meeting.

### Things not to say
    - Deliberate list — with reasons.
```

The plan is one page. It does not become the response;
it structures the drafting. In serious institutions the
plan is reviewed by the GC and the CRO before drafting
begins.

## Concrete example

A supervisor letter that lists four literal items —
model inventory, model risk policy, description of the
customer-service AI capability, and incident history for
that capability — is almost never *only* asking those
four things. The probable underlying concerns are:

- Is the AI in question actually covered by the
  institution's MRM framework, or has it slipped in
  through a customer-experience or product-marketing
  channel that MRM does not touch?
- If it is covered, did the coverage happen before or
  after the public announcement?
- Is the incident history a plausible reflection of what
  has happened, or a curated view?

A response that answers the four literal items and
ignores all three of those questions makes the underlying
concerns into follow-up questions on the record. A
response that addresses them proactively — through the
lede sentence, through structure, through the substance
of the answers to the literal items — resolves them
without requiring the regulator to escalate.

## Summary

- A regulator letter is a probe, not a questionnaire.
  Treat the literal items as the visible surface.
- Read for the underlying concern: why now, what recent
  event, what worry off-the-record.
- Reason about winning posture and anti-posture
  explicitly. Author them before drafting.
- The lede sentence is the frame the examiner will use
  for everything else. Author it deliberately.
- The one-page response plan structures the drafting.
  GC and CRO review the plan before the response is
  drafted.
- "Things not to say" is a discipline of not
  volunteering material that raises new concerns — never
  a licence to withhold answers to what was asked.
