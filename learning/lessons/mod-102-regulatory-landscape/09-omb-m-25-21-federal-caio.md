# Chapter 9 — OMB M-25-21 and the Federal Chief AI Officer

## Why this chapter exists

The US federal government runs one of the largest AI
deployment programs in the world through its agencies. In
2025 the Office of Management and Budget issued **M-25-21**
— *Accelerating Federal Use of Artificial Intelligence* —
which set the current shape of federal AI governance,
including the Chief AI Officer designation and the AI
Governance Board expected at every agency.

For a private-sector CAIRO the memo matters for three
reasons:

1. **Vocabulary.** M-25-21 uses *"Chief AI Officer"* to
   refer to the agency-designated senior official for AI.
   The private-sector title has migrated in the other
   direction — first appearing in the federal frame, then
   moving into industry. Understanding what the term
   originally meant clarifies what it does *not* mean in a
   private context.
2. **Structural equivalence.** The AI Governance Board
   required at every covered federal agency is
   structurally similar to the AI risk committee many
   private-sector CAIROs are building or inheriting. The
   parallels are useful when defending a proposed
   structure to a board or to an examiner.
3. **Government-facing exposure.** If your firm sells AI
   into federal agencies, the memo shapes what your
   customer must ask of you. Contracts, disclosures, and
   risk documentation trace back to it.

This chapter is deliberately shorter than the others. The
memo is dense but not conceptually complex; the CAIRO's job
is to know it exists, know what it requires, and know how
to position private-sector work against it.

## The shape of the memo

M-25-21 sits in a lineage of OMB AI memos going back to
M-21-06 (2020) and continuing through M-24-10 (2024). It
supersedes M-24-10 and sets the current baseline. Its
central moves:

- **Designation of a Chief AI Officer** at every covered
  federal agency, with the CAIO acting as the primary
  responsible official for coordinating agency AI use.
- **AI Governance Board** at every covered agency,
  chaired by the CAIO (or by a deputy secretary when the
  agency has one), with representation from the CIO,
  CDO, CISO, general counsel, and mission-area senior
  officials.
- **Annual agency AI strategy** and inventory of AI use
  cases published publicly, with defined categories for
  *high-impact* and *rights-impacting* AI.
- **Risk management requirements** for high-impact and
  rights-impacting AI, including pre-deployment testing,
  ongoing monitoring, and consultation with affected
  communities where appropriate.
- **Procurement provisions** to shape how agencies buy AI
  from private-sector vendors, including transparency
  obligations that flow to those vendors.

The memo distinguishes *"AI"* broadly (using the
AI-in-Government Act definition) from *"generative AI"*
(narrower, with its own provisions). It also distinguishes
*"high-impact AI"* and *"rights-impacting AI"* — categories
that carry heightened obligations analogous in spirit,
though not in text, to the EU AI Act's high-risk
category.

## The Federal CAIO as a role

The federal Chief AI Officer is a **senior official at the
agency**, typically at the Senior Executive Service (SES)
level, whose primary AI-coordination responsibilities
include:

- Serving as the primary point of contact within the
  agency for AI-related matters and coordinating with OMB.
- Advising senior leadership on AI strategy, risk, and
  investment.
- Chairing (or vice-chairing) the AI Governance Board.
- Overseeing the agency's AI use-case inventory and
  ensuring risk-management practices for high-impact and
  rights-impacting AI.
- Reporting on agency AI activities as required by the
  memo and by Congress.

Two observations for a private-sector CAIRO:

- The federal role is **coordinating, not
  operational**. The CAIO does not build systems; they
  coordinate the agency's use of them. This is closer to
  the second-line framing from mod-101 Chapter 3 than to
  a CTO / CIO framing. Private-sector CAIROs who fight for
  second-line placement have the federal role as a
  supportive analogy.
- The federal role is **statutorily anchored** to a
  senior agency officer whose accountabilities are
  externally defined. A private-sector CAIRO whose
  accountabilities are entirely internally defined and
  entirely subject to the CEO's discretion is in a
  weaker structural position. Where practical, private
  CAIROs pull toward externally-anchored accountabilities
  — regulator-facing filings, board-committee reporting
  lines, defined policy hierarchies — to shore up the
  position.

## The AI Governance Board as a structural template

M-25-21's AI Governance Board is a defined body with:

- **A chair** — the CAIO, or a deputy secretary in some
  configurations.
- **Named members by role** — CIO, CDO, CISO, GC, mission
  leaders.
- **A cadence** — typically not less than quarterly.
- **A remit** — reviewing high-impact AI use cases,
  overseeing the agency's AI strategy, resolving
  cross-cutting AI decisions, reviewing agency AI
  inventory and risk-management artifacts.
- **A reporting relationship** — outputs feed the
  agency's senior leadership and OMB.

For a private-sector CAIRO defending a proposed AI risk
committee to a board, the federal structure is a useful
analogy in three ways:

1. **Named members by role.** Not open-ended. Not
   *"whoever wants to attend."* A board with a defined
   membership can be governed; an open forum cannot.
2. **A defined remit.** The committee decides specific
   categories of question — deployment approvals for
   high-impact systems, risk-appetite exceptions, incident
   escalations. Committees without defined remits become
   status meetings.
3. **A defined chair with accountability elsewhere.**
   The CAIO chairs the federal board, and the CAIO
   reports out to the agency head. Private-sector AI
   committees chaired by anyone whose primary
   accountability is not AI drift into information sharing
   rather than decision making.

The equivalence is structural, not textual. Do not cite
M-25-21 in a private-sector policy document; the memo
does not apply. Cite the structural pattern.

## What M-25-21 is *not*

Three misreads to avoid:

- **It is not a private-sector rule.** M-25-21 governs
  covered federal executive-branch agencies and, through
  procurement, agency vendors. It does not create
  obligations for private-sector AI deployment generally.
- **It is not the source of the private-sector Chief AI
  Officer title.** The private-sector title developed
  alongside the federal one. The federal memo shapes what
  the term means in government; the private-sector role
  is defined by the firm's board, charter, and
  operational context.
- **It is not equivalent to the EU AI Act.** The memo
  governs federal use, not market placement. A vendor
  can be compliant with M-25-21 procurement provisions
  and non-compliant with the EU AI Act, and vice versa.
  The two regimes overlap thematically but sit in
  different regulatory frames.

## Vendor exposure

If your firm sells AI to federal agencies, three
procurement-side considerations flow from M-25-21:

- **Transparency obligations.** Agencies increasingly
  require vendor disclosure of AI system capabilities,
  limitations, and testing. Contracts include specific
  transparency clauses; sales cycles include AI-related
  due diligence.
- **Rights-impacting categorisation.** If your product is
  used in a rights-impacting way at the agency, the
  agency must apply heightened controls. Your product
  documentation should support that categorisation.
- **Federal inventory publication.** Agencies publish
  their AI use-case inventories. Your product appearing
  in the inventory is public; the description of it in
  that inventory is public. Review how agencies describe
  your product.

For firms that sell to both federal and EU customers, the
transparency artifacts overlap enough that a single set
of documentation can serve both — with the caveats about
frame differences noted above. Chapter 6's obligations
register is the mechanism for keeping this coherent.

## Summary

- OMB M-25-21 governs federal use of AI and defines the
  Chief AI Officer designation, AI Governance Board, and
  agency AI inventory obligations.
- The federal CAIO is a coordinating role at the SES
  level, with statutorily-anchored accountabilities —
  useful as an analogy when positioning a private-sector
  CAIRO in a second-line structural role.
- The federal AI Governance Board is a useful structural
  template for private-sector AI risk committees: named
  membership, defined remit, defined chair, defined
  cadence.
- The memo is not a private-sector rule and is not the
  EU AI Act. Do not cite it as authority in a private
  context; cite the structural pattern.
- If you sell AI to federal agencies, the memo shapes
  procurement obligations and transparency artifacts.
  Cross-map to your obligations register.
